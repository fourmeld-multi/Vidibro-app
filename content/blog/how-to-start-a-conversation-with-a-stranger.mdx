The first three seconds of a random chat set the tone for everything after it. Most people open with "hi" or "how are you," which isn't wrong exactly — it's just forgettable, and forgettable openers tend to produce forgettable replies. Here's what tends to actually work.

## Lead with something specific, not a category

"What's up" invites another "not much." A specific, slightly odd question invites an actual answer: "What's the last thing that made you laugh today?" or "What's open near you right now?" Specificity does the work that a generic greeting can't — it gives the other person something concrete to respond to instead of a blank prompt.

## Use what's actually visible

If it's video, you're not starting from nothing — there's a background, a time of day, maybe a T-shirt with something on it. "Is that a guitar behind you?" is a better opener than anything generic, because it's true and it's about them specifically, not a script you'd send to anyone.

## Silence isn't failure

A pause after your first message doesn't mean the conversation is dying. People type, think, or just take a second to decide if they want to engage — that's normal, not a rejection. Give it a beat before assuming it's not working.

## If it's not clicking, that's fine — that's what Next is for

Not every match is going to be a good conversation, and that's not a reflection on you or them. It's genuinely fine to hit Next after ten seconds if it's clearly not going anywhere. The whole design of random chat assumes most matches won't be the one — it's built for trying again quickly, not for forcing one conversation to work.

## Match their energy, don't overshoot it

If someone's replying in short, low-energy messages, a wall of enthusiastic text can feel like a lot. Starting at their pace and building from there tends to land better than coming in at full intensity from message one.

## A few openers that reliably fall flat

Not every opener is equal, and some patterns tend to kill a conversation before it starts. Anything that requires a one-word answer — "good?" "nice weather?" — hands the other person nothing to build on. Compliments about appearance before any actual conversation has happened tend to read as the whole point of the interaction rather than a natural observation. And a question you'd clearly send to literally anyone ("so... how's your day") signals that you're not actually paying attention to who you were matched with, even if that's not the intent.

## If you're nervous about being on camera

Camera nerves are common and don't mean video chat isn't for you — they usually fade within the first minute of an actual conversation, once you're focused on what's being said rather than how you look saying it. If it doesn't fade, that's useful information too: voice-only keeps the same live, real-time feel without the camera, and text removes the live-response pressure entirely. None of these is a downgrade from video — they're just different tools, and starting with whichever one lowers your nerves enough to actually engage is a better strategy than forcing video because it feels like the "main" option.

## The best conversations aren't planned

Some of the best exchanges on a platform like this happen by accident — a tangent neither person expected, a shared specific interest that comes up randomly. You can't script your way into that. The goal of a good opener isn't to control where the conversation goes; it's just to get it started so something real has room to happen.
